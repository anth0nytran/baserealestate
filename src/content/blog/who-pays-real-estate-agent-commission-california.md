This is the question I get asked more than any other, and it is the one where the answer changed most recently. If you last bought a home before 2024, what you remember is no longer how it works.

Two separate changes landed within about five months of each other: the national practice changes from the National Association of REALTORS® settlement in August 2024, and a California statute that took effect on 1 January 2025. Together they moved buyer-agent compensation from something assumed and invisible to something written down and negotiated before you tour a property.

Here is what that actually means.

## Who pays the buyer's agent in California now?

The buyer is contractually responsible for their agent's compensation, in an amount agreed in writing before the offer. In practice, that amount is very often still paid by the seller as a negotiated concession — but it is no longer assumed, and it is no longer advertised.

The distinction matters. Before 2024, a seller's listing broker published an offer of compensation to buyer brokers on the MLS. A buyer could go through an entire purchase without ever discussing what their agent was paid, because it was arranged between brokerages in the background.

That is gone. Compensation is now a term you negotiate — first with your own agent, and then, separately, with the seller.

## What does California Civil Code §1670.50 require?

California Assembly Bill 2992 added §1670.50 to the Civil Code, effective 1 January 2025. It requires a written buyer-broker representation agreement, and it specifies what that agreement must contain.

| Requirement | What the statute says |
| --- | --- |
| When it must be signed | As soon as practicable, and no later than the execution of the buyer's offer to purchase |
| Compensation | The amount to be paid to the broker must be stated |
| Services | The services the broker will provide must be described |
| Timing | When the compensation becomes due must be stated |
| Term | Maximum 90 days, unless the buyer is a corporation, LLC, or partnership |

The 90-day cap is worth noting. You are not signing an open-ended commitment. If the relationship is not working, it expires on its own.

The statute applies broadly across real property — one-to-four units, larger residential, commercial, vacant land, manufactured homes, and ground leases with improvements. It does not apply to leases or rental agreements.

## What changed under the NAR settlement?

Two things, effective 17 August 2024:

- **Offers of compensation can no longer be published on the MLS.** Listing brokers may still offer to compensate a buyer's broker, but that offer has to be communicated some other way, and it is negotiated rather than broadcast.
- **Buyers must have a written agreement with their agent before touring a home** when working with an MLS-participating agent.

California's §1670.50 then went further, adding statutory content requirements and the 90-day term limit.

## So can the seller still pay my agent?

Yes. This is the single most common misunderstanding, and it costs buyers money when they assume otherwise.

Sellers may still contribute toward the buyer's agent compensation. It is now handled as a **concession negotiated in the purchase agreement** rather than an offer published on the MLS. Sellers frequently agree, because a seller who declines narrows their own buyer pool.

What changed is the mechanism and the visibility, not the possibility. Your agreement with your agent establishes what you owe; the negotiation with the seller establishes how much of that the seller covers. If the seller covers all of it, your out-of-pocket cost is unchanged from the old system.

## What are commissions actually costing in 2026?

Commission rates are not regulated in California, and the NAR settlement did not introduce caps. Rates are negotiable, and they always were — the settlement made that negotiability explicit rather than creating it.

Published national and state averages in 2026 put total commissions in the mid-five-percent range, typically divided between the listing side and the buyer side, with listing-side figures commonly quoted around two to three percent. Treat those as orientation, not as a quote. What you pay is what your agreement says.

What I would tell you to focus on instead of the headline percentage:

1. **What the agreement obligates you to.** Read the compensation clause and the "when is it due" clause. Those two sentences are the whole financial commitment.
2. **What happens if the seller pays less than your agreed amount.** A well-drafted agreement addresses the gap. Ask directly what happens if the seller offers less than the agreed figure, and get the answer before you sign.
3. **What you are getting for it.** The statute requires the services to be described. That description is the part most buyers skim and the part that most determines whether the fee was worth paying.

## Does this change anything for sellers?

Less than most sellers expect, but one thing genuinely matters: whether to offer a concession toward the buyer's agent.

You are no longer obliged to, and you cannot advertise it on the MLS. But buyers now arrive with a signed agreement stating what they owe their agent. If your listing offers no contribution and a comparable one does, some buyers will find the comparable one materially cheaper to transact on — and in a market where active listings are at a yearly high, that is a real disadvantage.

This is a pricing decision like any other, and it should be made with the numbers in front of you rather than on principle.

## What should you do before you sign anything?

Ask your agent to walk you through the agreement line by line before you are emotionally attached to a property. That conversation takes twenty minutes and is the single highest-return twenty minutes in the whole transaction.

Specifically, get plain answers to:

- What is the compensation figure, and how is it calculated?
- When does it become due, and under what circumstances?
- What happens if the seller contributes less than that figure?
- What services am I getting, in writing?
- What is the term, and how do I end it early?

If those answers are not straightforward, that is information too.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Who pays the buyer's agent commission in California in 2026?

The buyer is contractually responsible under a written buyer-broker representation agreement, which California Civil Code §1670.50 has required since 1 January 2025. In practice, sellers still frequently cover some or all of that amount as a concession negotiated in the purchase agreement, so many buyers pay nothing out of pocket. The difference from the old system is that it is negotiated and written down rather than assumed.

### Do I have to sign a buyer-broker agreement before seeing a house in California?

Yes. Under the NAR settlement practice changes effective 17 August 2024, buyers must have a written agreement with an MLS-participating agent before touring a home. California Civil Code §1670.50 additionally requires the agreement to be signed no later than when you execute an offer, and to state the compensation, the services, when payment is due, and the term.

### How long does a California buyer-broker agreement last?

No longer than 90 days, unless the buyer is a corporation, limited liability company, or partnership. The statutory cap means an individual buyer is never locked into an open-ended commitment.

### Are real estate commissions negotiable in California?

Yes. California does not regulate commission rates and the NAR settlement imposed no caps. Compensation is negotiated between you and your broker and stated in your written agreement. It is also negotiated separately with the seller, who may agree to cover part or all of it as a concession.

### Can a seller refuse to pay the buyer's agent?

Yes. Sellers are under no obligation to contribute. However, buyers now arrive knowing exactly what they owe their own agent, so a listing that offers no contribution can be more expensive for a buyer to transact on than a comparable one that does. With inventory at a yearly high in Orange County, that can matter.

### What is the average real estate commission in California in 2026?

Published 2026 averages put total commissions in the mid-five-percent range statewide, commonly split between listing and buyer sides. These are averages, not standards, and they are not binding on any transaction. Your cost is whatever your written agreement states.
